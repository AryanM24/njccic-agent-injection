NJCCIC Strategic Plan 2026-2030

Protecting New Jersey’s public and private institutions, critical infrastructure assets, and residents from cyber threats requires a unified, sustained, and strategic approach - one that has been central to the mission of the NJCCIC since its inception.
  • Vision Statement
  • Mission Statement
  • Core Values

A safe, secure, and resilient New Jersey that is able to fully realize the opportunities and benefits of technological innovations that act as an engine for economic growth and societal gains.

To lead and coordinate New Jersey’s cybersecurity efforts while building resiliency to cyber threats throughout the State.

SERVICE. We put our State and its citizens first, and we put Mission before self. We take pride in being timely, agile, and relevant.

TEAMWORK. We stand with and behind each other. We recognize that partnerships, both internal and external, are critical to achieving success. We cannot fulfill our Mission alone.

EXCELLENCE. We take great pride in the quality of our work. We do every task, every project, every initiative, to the best of our ability.

DIVERSITY. We strive to build a workforce that is as diverse as New Jersey’s citizenry. We pride ourselves on encouraging diversity of thought, perspective, and problem solving.

INTEGRITY. We are committed to holding ourselves accountable to the highest moral and ethical standards in our personal and professional conduct. We can be relied upon to act with honor and truthfulness.

Introduction

Established in 2015 within the New Jersey Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness, the New Jersey Cybersecurity and Communications Integration Cell (NJCCIC) is charged with leading and coordinating all aspects of the State's cybersecurity efforts. In September 2020, the NJCCIC published its inaugural 2021–2025 NJCCIC Cybersecurity Strategic Plan, which charted a path toward improved cyber readiness and collective defense across New Jersey. It formalized a “whole-of-state” approach, enhanced cybersecurity leadership through the NJCCIC, initiated capability-building efforts, and fostered partnerships across public and private sectors. Key achievements under that plan included growth of the NJCCIC as a cybersecurity center of excellence, capability building initiatives to bolster cyber resilience in state and local government entities and municipal water utilities, dedicated efforts to improve election security, the expansion of cybersecurity education and training, among others. These successes have established a foundation for ongoing collaboration and innovation, significantly bolstering New Jersey's digital resilience and preparedness.

This strategic plan is a renewed, forward-looking roadmap to ensure New Jersey remains a safe, resilient, and innovative leader in an age of rapid technological changes, an expanding attack surface, a growingly complex and pervasive threat environment, government policy changes, and a highly combustible geopolitical environment. It acknowledges that cybersecurity is not merely defensive but an enabler of progress and prosperity for New Jersey and the United States. The plan proactively addresses today’s cyber challenges and prepares for those of tomorrow, calling for unified commitment, strategic investment, and steadfast vigilance to secure New Jersey’s digital future.

The Evolving Digital Landscape
The cyber threat landscape has not remained static. In fact, it has changed dramatically over the past five years. The 2026-2030 NJCCIC Strategic is informed by three converging forces that define our current challenge.

Expanding Attack Surface

The rapid digital transformation of modern society has created an unprecedented and continuously expanding attack surface, increasingly exploited by sophisticated adversaries who operate without geographic boundaries or temporal limitations. This interconnected ecosystem now spans cloud infrastructure, operational technology, mobile devices, and billions of Internet of Things (IoT) endpoints, all potential entry points for malicious actors. Incidents such as the 2024 Change Healthcare cyberattack - that exposed sensitive data on more than 190 million Americans, including over 4 million New Jersey residents - and cyberattacks targeting New Jersey municipal water systems, hospitals, local governments, and schools underscore the magnitude of the threat. These events reveal not only the scale of the State’s digital exposure but also the profound risk such attacks pose to essential services, public trust, and the safety and well-being of New Jersey residents.

Technological Acceleration

The exponential pace of technological advancement has fundamentally transformed the threat landscape, creating asymmetric advantages for adversaries that present significant challenges for defenders. Artificial intelligence now enables attackers to generate highly convincing phishing campaigns, create deepfakes for fraud and disinformation, and develop malware that adapts in real-time to evade detection. Meanwhile, the approaching quantum computing era threatens to render current encryption methods obsolete, requiring a comprehensive transition to post-quantum cryptography within this decade.

Shifting Federal Priorities

In March 2025, the White House issued Executive Order 14239, “Achieving Efficiency Through State and Local Preparedness,” which fundamentally rebalances cybersecurity roles across the nation. Whereas previous approaches positioned the federal government as the primary defender, the new model shifts primary responsibility for cyber preparedness, defense, and response to state, local, and private sector entities, with federal agencies serving in a supporting and coordination role.

The defunding of the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center (MS-ISAC) further underscores this shift and highlights the urgency for New Jersey to accelerate efforts to build a more robust, self-sufficient cybersecurity ecosystem capable of protecting critical services and infrastructure with reduced federal support.

Threat Environment
New Jersey’s threat environment for the foreseeable future is marked by a persistent, dynamic, and increasingly sophisticated array of cyber adversaries and risks. The NJCCIC’s 2025 Cyber Threat Assessment highlights several key trends shaping the State’s risk landscape.

Nation-State Adversaries

State-sponsored cyber actors from China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and others persistently target U.S. companies and critical infrastructure to advance military, political, and economic agendas. For example, China’s Volt Typhoon operation has been prepositioning access in U.S. energy, water, transportation, and healthcare networks aiming to disrupt critical services in a conflict scenario. Russia’s Midnight Blizzard (APT29/Cozy Bear) conducts sophisticated espionage, exemplified by the 2023 compromise of Microsoft email systems affecting government and corporate environments. These advanced persistent threats demonstrate the capability to maintain long-term, stealthy access to vital networks. The consequence of such intrusions could be disabling of power grids, disruption of transit and water systems, and widespread chaos. Nation-states pose a grave long-term threat to state and national security, and New Jersey’s critical infrastructure sectors - energy, water, healthcare, transportation, finance, and more - remain prime targets for disruption.

Ransomware and Cybercrime Escalation

Financially motivated cybercriminal groups continue to carry out lucrative yet debilitating ransomware attacks against a broad spectrum of targets – from businesses of all sizes to schools, hospitals, and municipal governments. Data compiled by the NJCCIC from ransomware groups’ public leak sites indicates a continued upward trend in global ransomware activity. The NJCCIC identified 3,640 attacks in 2022, 4,591 in 2023, and 5,289 in 2024. As of September 2025, 4,701 incidents had already been recorded representing a sharp increase over the same period in 2024. The impact is severe; for instance, a February 2024 attack on Change Healthcare not only encrypted systems but also exfiltrated 6 terabytes of sensitive data (health and personal information on over 190 million individuals), resulting in an unprecedented healthcare data breach. The company paid a $22 million ransom and incurred millions more in recovery costs. Ransomware attacks targeting organizations as diverse as the State of Nevada, the City of Saint Paul, the New York Blood Center, Veolia, Caeser’s Entertainment, and Marks & Spencer demonstrate that the threat transcends sectors and geographies, inflicting severe operational and financial consequences across both public and private institutions. New Jersey’s public, private, and community organizations continue to face relentless malware campaigns, data breaches, and extortion schemes.

Compromised Credentials as a Primary Attack Vector

Stolen or weak credentials remain one of the most prevalent initial access vectors in cyber intrusions. Threat actors frequently obtain usernames and passwords via phishing campaigns, malware (infostealers), or data breaches, and then use those credentials to bypass authentication and penetrate networks. Reports indicate over 15 billion sets of compromised credentials are circulating on the internet – a staggering number that underscores the scale of this problem. High-profile incidents, from the 2021 Colonial Pipeline breach to recent attacks on New Jersey entities, often trace back to password compromises. Malware like infostealers can silently harvest login credentials from infected endpoints, which attackers later leverage for unauthorized access and lateral movement. Since 2020, the NJCCIC has proactively identified and notified public-sector users of leaked credentials to help them take protective action. “Credential stuffing” (testing stolen logins against various services) and “password spraying” attacks (brute force attack where malicious actors attempt to use the same password on multiple accounts) are rampant. Because compromised credentials typically open the door for attackers, improving authentication (through measures like multi-factor authentication and phishing-resistant methods) is one of the most urgent security priorities. This attack vector is expected to remain a top choice for threat actors, requiring constant vigilance by all organizations.

Hacktivist and Cybercriminal Alliances

The once-distinct lines between nation-states, cybercriminals, and hacktivists have blurred as these groups increasingly collaborate and share tools. Over the past two years, evidence has emerged of Russian ransomware gangs and hacktivist groups acting as proxies for their state intelligence services. Notably, the Cyber Army of Russia Reborn (CARR), which now calls itself the Z-Pentest Alliance, was tasked by Russia’s GRU intelligence agency to attack Western targets in 2024. These groups even compromised industrial control systems of multiple U.S. water, energy, and manufacturing facilities - including four municipal water systems in New Jersey - demonstrating that politically motivated hackers can now threaten critical infrastructure on a large scale. Another prolific group, NoName-57(16), has conducted widespread DDoS attacks disrupting government and private websites in the U.S. and allied countries. Similarly, Iranian hacktivists (e.g. CyberAv3ngers) operate with support from Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). Even in the criminal underworld, new partnerships are being forged – for example, Western financially motivated hackers teaming up with Russian ransomware operators. New hacktivist or criminal alliances are announced almost daily on Telegram, aligning diverse threat actors around mutual goals and complicating attribution for attacks. The implication for New Jersey is a more complex threat matrix, where ideologically driven attacks (disruption, DDoS, espionage, and financial crime) may intersect.

AI and Quantum Disruption

The rapid evolution of AI introduces new dimensions to the threat landscape. Threat actors are already using AI to conduct reconnaissance, develop malware, evade detection, and manipulate public opinion. Deepfakes and AI-generated disinformation are eroding public trust and posing threats to democratic processes, while AI-assisted attack automation enables adversaries to scale operations beyond human capacity further exacerbating the asymmetric advantage attackers hold. Quantum computing presents another strategic risk. While practical, large-scale quantum systems are still years away, their eventual capability to break widely used encryption algorithms represents an existential challenge to digital security. Planning for a “post-quantum” future — including the adoption of quantum-resistant cryptography — must begin now.

Systemic Risk and Supply Chain Vulnerabilities

Modern society’s reliance on interconnected technologies has created systemic cyber risks capable of causing cascading failures across multiple sectors. The 2024 CrowdStrike update failure, which caused 8.5 million devices to crash globally, demonstrated how a single software issue can cripple essential services. Similarly, the NotPetya attack in 2017 and the Viasat hack by Russia's intelligence services illustrated how targeted cyber operations can have far-reaching, unintended consequences. Recent failures of Microsoft, Google, and Amazon cloud platforms have made clear that even the most trusted digital backbones are single points of systemic risk, capable of disrupting everything from government operations to critical infrastructure within seconds.

Overall Threat Outlook

In sum, New Jersey faces a heightened threat environment: nation-state advanced persistent threat (APT) actors prepositioning in our infrastructure; relentless ransomware and cybercrime campaigns; emerging threats from AI-enabled tactics; ubiquitous credential theft; and an assorted mix of adversaries often working in concert. These trends underscore an imperative for New Jersey to elevate its cybersecurity posture by anticipating advanced threats, hardening our defenses, and ensuring swift, unified responses to incidents.

New Jersey's Approach to Cybersecurity
New Jersey leads the nation in three critical dimensions: it is the most densely populated state, the most digitally connected, and home to the highest concentration of critical infrastructure in the nation.

This extensive interconnectivity - spanning energy, water, transportation, healthcare, education, finance, and community and government services - creates an ecosystem of both unparalleled opportunity and heightened systemic risk. A cyber incident affecting any one component of this ecosystem has the potential to generate cascading impacts across multiple sectors, disrupting essential services, degrading operational continuity, and threatening public health, safety, and economic stability. In such an environment, a single point of compromise can precipitate widespread consequences, underscoring the imperative for coordinated and collaborative, whole-of-state cybersecurity collective resilience.

Whole-of-State Collaboration

The NJCCIC serves as the State’s central coordinating body for implementing this unified approach: leading strategy, facilitating information sharing, and enabling collective defense across the public and private sectors. Recognizing the State’s deep interdependencies, New Jersey is advancing a comprehensive whole-of-state cybersecurity model that aligns strategy, resources, and capabilities across all levels of government, industry, and civil society. This approach moves beyond isolated initiatives toward an integrated framework that strengthens statewide resilience and ensures every community - regardless of size, resources, or technical maturity - benefits from shared security capacity. By leading and coordinating cybersecurity efforts under this model, the State can more effectively anticipate, withstand, and recover from cyber incidents that threaten the continuity of essential functions. Through sustained partnerships among state and local governments, critical infrastructure operators, educational institutions, and private-sector and community organizations, New Jersey is building a resilient digital ecosystem capable of protecting its people, its economy, and its future.

Operationalizing Whole-of-State Collaboration

This collaborative framework is operationalized through a series of coordinated mechanisms that align people, processes, and technology across the State’s cybersecurity ecosystem. The NJCCIC leads these efforts by facilitating the bidirectional exchange of actionable intelligence between state and federal agencies, local governments, educational entities, critical infrastructure operators, private-sector partners, community organizations, and the general public, ensuring situational awareness and rapid dissemination of threat information. Sector-based partnerships enhance mutual support and enable a unified response posture during cyber incidents.

The NJCCIC delivers enterprise-grade cybersecurity capabilities tailored to the needs of public-sector organizations across New Jersey. They include comprehensive defense-in-depth prevention, detection, and response tools, technologies, and services for all executive branch agencies. Using funding from several state and federal grant programs, the NJCCIC can provide advanced protections to other state and local government organizations, K-12 school systems, higher education institutions, and municipal water systems that might otherwise lack the resources to defend themselves effectively.

The NJCCIC’s continuous statewide attack surface and risk management services, cybersecurity workforce training, and joint exercises further strengthen the State’s preparedness, interoperability, and collective resilience. Together, these efforts establish a scalable model of statewide cybersecurity collaboration and coordination, necessary for whole-of-state cyber defense. By centralizing these shared capabilities, the State reduces operational fragmentation, achieves economies of scale, and ensures consistent standards of protection.

Advancing Statewide Cyber Resilience

As New Jersey continues to mature its whole-of-state cybersecurity framework, the next imperative is to translate strategic coordination into measurable defensive capability. Strengthening statewide cyber resilience requires not only the integration of advanced technologies and adaptive risk management practices, but also the mobilization of partnerships that extend across every level of government and the community. This holistic approach recognizes that true resilience is achieved not through isolated efforts, but through collective defense - linking state agencies, county and municipal governments, educational institutions, critical infrastructure operators, the private sector, and civil society under a unified collaborative model. Building on the foundation of whole-of-state coordination and shared situational awareness established through the NJCCIC, New Jersey will continue to expand its defensive depth through coordinated service delivery, shared visibility, and cross-sector collaboration. This approach reduces operational fragmentation, achieves economies of scale, and ensures consistent standards of protection statewide. By harmonizing cybersecurity operations, policies, and communications across jurisdictions, New Jersey can deploy defenses more efficiently, respond more rapidly to incidents, and adapt more effectively to evolving threats.

A cornerstone of this evolution is the New Jersey Cyber Resilience Corps, a pioneering initiative designed to augment local and state cybersecurity capacity through volunteer service. Drawing from a diverse pool of professionals, students, and community members, the Corps provides surge support to under-resourced organizations, assists with cyber hygiene and incident response activities, and fosters a culture of civic cyber engagement. The program exemplifies the whole-of-state model in action - transforming individual expertise into collective strength while building a sustainable talent pipeline for the future.

Together, these initiatives operationalize New Jersey’s vision of true whole-of-state cybersecurity: a federated ecosystem in which every connected entity contributes to a unified, adaptive defense posture through continuous collaboration, information sharing, and coordinated response. The following strategic goals and objectives outline the actions necessary to achieve this transformation, fortify statewide resilience, and sustain New Jersey’s standing as a national leader in cyber resilience.

 


“Despite the cyber field’s tendency to focus on each sector individually, or to prioritize only those that qualify as “critical infrastructure,” community organizations are inherently interconnected and interdependent, meaning that disruption in services in one can have cascading effects on others. Goals, Objectives, and Action Items: Strategic Goal 1

STRATEGIC GOAL 1: CYBERSECURITY LEADERSHIP AND COORDINATED GOVERNANCE

Lead, coordinate, and enable a true whole-of-state cybersecurity ecosystem that empowers every government entity, academic institution, business, community organization, and citizen to contribute to New Jersey's cyber resilience.

Objective 1.1

Establish and grow the NJCCIC as New Jersey’s Cybersecurity Center of Excellence (CCOE) that provides leadership, best practices, training, support, and research.

Action Items:

  1. Expand NJCCIC’s leadership role as a trusted statewide authority on cybersecurity best practices, systemic risk management, and emerging technologies.
  2. Create sector-specific guidance tailored to state and local government entities, K-12 and higher education institutions, industry and small businesses, nonprofits, healthcare, and critical infrastructure.
  3. Develop scalable cybersecurity frameworks with small, medium, and large implementation pathways that guide organizations in building and improving their security capabilities.
  4. Establish the Cyber Secure New Jersey certification program, aligned to these pathways, to provide baseline, intermediate, and advanced recognition as organizations progress along the framework.
  5. Provide actionable threat intelligence through technical feeds, plain-language advisories, and sector-specific alerts.
  6. Convene a Cybersecurity Leadership Council representing government, industry, academia, small business, community organizations, and other sectors to align statewide priorities.

Objective 1.2

Champion a culture of cybersecurity awareness and responsibility across all sectors.

Action Items:

  1. As appropriate, partner with and integrate third-party cybersecurity organizations' materials into NJCCIC-provided cybersecurity awareness, guidance, and best practices products.
  2. Launch a Cyber Aware NJ campaign promoting practical cybersecurity steps for individuals and families.
  3. Develop toolkits and guidance materials tailored to organizations with limited cybersecurity resources.
  4. Partner with education, community, and business associations to integrate cybersecurity into organizational governance and daily operations.
  5. Partner with libraries, senior centers, chambers of commerce, and faith-based groups to deliver local cybersecurity education.
  6. Integrate cybersecurity into existing state programs such as workforce development, small business assistance, economic development, and community organization initiatives.
  7. Create multilingual, culturally tailored resources to reach New Jersey’s diverse population.
  8. Encourage adoption of widely recognized cybersecurity frameworks (e.g., National Institute of Science and Technology (NIST) Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls) across all sectors.

OBJECTIVE 1.3

Prepare for and adapt to an evolving threat landscape.

Action Items:

  1. Maintain continuous threat intelligence collection and analysis capabilities to identify emerging threat actors, attack techniques, and vulnerability trends before they impact New Jersey.
  2. Develop adaptive security frameworks that can rapidly incorporate new defensive techniques, respond to novel attack vectors, and evolve with changing threats.
  3. Create rapid response capabilities to address zero-day vulnerabilities, emerging threats, and novel attack techniques.
  4. Monitor geopolitical developments, international conflicts, and nation-state cyber operations to anticipate impacts on New Jersey.
  5. Prepare for heightened threat periods including the 2026 FIFA World Cup, Sail 4th 250, and other high-profile events with enhanced monitoring and response capabilities.
  6. Foster a culture of innovation within the NJCCIC that encourages experimentation, accepts calculated risk, learns from failures, and rewards creative problem-solving.
  7. Update and align New Jersey's cybersecurity strategy regularly to meet evolving threats and societal needs.

OBJECTIVE 1.4

Address security challenges of expanding attack surface and emerging technologies.

Action Items:

  1. Develop comprehensive strategies and tactics to address threats introduced by continued digital transformation, including artificial intelligence (AI), IoT proliferation, 5G expansion, smart cities, autonomous systems, and edge computing.
  2. Establish security requirements and testing procedures for IoT devices procured by state government and recommended for local government and critical infrastructure partners.
  3. Develop guidance and support for securing operational technology and industrial control systems used in critical infrastructure.
  4. Develop smart city security frameworks that address interconnected systems including traffic management, building automation, environmental monitoring, and public safety systems.
  5. Research security implications of emerging technologies including autonomous vehicles, drones, robotics, and other innovations with potential government or critical infrastructure applications.
  6. Partner with academic institutions and the private sector on research and development of security solutions for emerging technologies.

Objective 1.5

Strengthen Cybersecurity Governance, Accountability, and Resilience Across Government.

Action Items:

  1. Update the Statewide Information Security Manual to include CSF 2.0 components along with AI governance, supply chain risk, post-quantum planning, zero-trust requirements, cloud security baselines, data classification, handling, and privacy protection standards, and incident reporting and response protocols aligned with P.L. 2023, c.19.
  2. Implement continuous cybersecurity assessments and standardized maturity models for all executive branch agencies.
  3. Establish clear incident reporting and response protocols aligned with P.L. 2023, c.19.
  4. Develop executive-level cybersecurity education for state leaders, emphasizing cyber risk as enterprise risk.
  5. Develop and provide cybersecurity performance metrics to state agency leadership.
  6. Conduct statewide capability assessments and publish cyber resilience reports to track progress and inform investment priorities.

Objective 1.6

Advance cybersecurity education and workforce development to build New Jersey's cyber talent pipeline.

Action Items:

  1. Expand K-12 cybersecurity education with age-appropriate curricula and teacher training in partnership with the NJ Department of Education.
  2. Grow participation in hands-on competitions such as Jersey Capture-the-Flag (CTF), Garden State CTF, and Cyber Patriot.
  3. Align higher education degree programs with workforce needs and promote industry-aligned certifications.
  4. Work with the legislature and Governor's office to establish a NJ Cyber Scholars Program offering scholarships in exchange for public service commitments.
  5. Develop and expand NJCCIC internship and fellowship programs to include year-round internship opportunities with pathways to employment, graduate fellowships for advanced research projects, and partnerships with universities for work-study and co-op programs.
  6. Partner with the NJ Department of Labor and Workforce Development to launch statewide apprenticeship, internship, and reskilling programs to transition IT professionals and career-changers into cybersecurity roles.
  7. Partner with the NJ Civil Service Commission to establish cybersecurity job classifications and career pathways.

Objective 1.7

Support legislative initiatives and pursue sustainable funding to strengthen New Jersey's cybersecurity posture.

Action Items:

  1. Provide regular threat briefings and strategic recommendations to the Governor’s Office, legislature, and key committees.
  2. Support legislation on cyber incident reporting, critical infrastructure protection, data privacy, and secure-bydesign and secure-by-default principles.
  3. Develop budget justifications and return on investment analysis for cybersecurity spending.
  4. Advocate for sustained, recurring baseline funding for NJCCIC operations, capital investments for cyber defense technology upgrades and modernization, state cybersecurity grants programs for local government and critical infrastructure, and workforce development funding.
  5. Track and pursue all available federal funding opportunities including but not limited to U.S Department of Homeland Security grants, Department of Justice grants, National Institute of Science and Technology (NIST) grants, National Science Foundation (NSF) grants, and other federal funding programs.
  6. Monitor federal policy changes (e.g., Executive Order 14239) and develop state-level responses to address capability gaps.
  7. Coordinate with neighboring states on model legislation, reciprocal aid agreements, and joint incident response frameworks.
  8. Develop strategic roadmaps to guide adoption of secure-by-design principles, zero-trust architectures, and future ready security models across public and private sectors.

Objective 1.8

Measure and Continuously Improve Cyber Posture.

Action Items:

  1. Develop metrics and performance indicators to assess statewide cybersecurity readiness and resilience.
  2. Conduct periodic capability assessments.
  3. Use threat intelligence and analytics to inform strategic priorities and investment decisions.
  4. Publish a Cybersecurity Threat Assessment and a State of Cybersecurity report annually.

Goals, Objectives, and Action Items: Strategic Goal 2

STRATEGIC GOAL 2: STRENGTHEN STATEWIDE CYBERSECURITY DEFENSES AND RESILIENCE

Build resilient, adaptive defenses that protect state and local government assets, critical infrastructure, and essential services; enable rapid detection, response, and recovery; and prepare New Jersey for emerging technological threats.

Objective 2.1

Deploy Advanced, Risk-Based Cybersecurity Defenses Across Government and Critical Infrastructure.

Action Items:

  1. Deploy zero trust architectures across state networks and promote adoption across local government and critical infrastructure sectors.
  2. Expand deployment of managed security services (EDR/MDR, SIEM, threat hunting, vulnerability scanning, identity management, email security, and cloud posture management) to state and local government entities.
  3. Establish a Shared Cybersecurity Services Model to centralize SOC operations, threat intelligence, vulnerability scanning, and identity/access management, achieving economies of scale and eliminating duplicative investments.
  4. Conduct and coordinate risk and vulnerability assessments for government entities, critical infrastructure and key resources (CIKR), owners and operators, academic institutions, small businesses, and civil society and community organizations.
  5. Mature the supply chain security program, including vendor risk assessments, continuous monitoring, and incident notification requirements.
  6. Create and enforce secure configuration baselines and baseline OT/ICS security controls to protect critical infrastructure systems.
  7. Coordinate efforts to combat cyber-enabled fraud.
  8. Establish Cyber Resilience Standards for critical infrastructure operators with implementation support and compliance incentives.
  9. Promote adoption of secure software development practices and secure procurement requirements statewide.

Objective 2.2

Enhance Cyber Incident Detection, Response, and Recovery Capabilities.

Action Items:

  1. Enhance the NJCCIC Security Operations Center (SOC) with 24/7 coverage, AI-powered analytics, and automated threat detection and response capabilities.
  2. Establish and deploy Cyber Incident Response Teams (CIRTs) comprising state and local government, private sector, National Guard, and volunteer personnel to respond to significant incidents.
  3. Activate and operationalize the NJ Civilian Cyber Resilience Corps to provide rapid surge support for resourceconstrained organizations.
  4. Develop and regularly update incident response playbooks for ransomware, data breaches, Industrial Control Systems (ICS)/Operational Technology (OT) disruptions, and other high-impact scenarios. Integrate cyber response into the State Emergency Operations Plan, including updates to the Cybersecurity Incident Annex to reflect a whole-of-state approach.
  5. Conduct statewide cyber exercises and simulations involving public and private sector stakeholders to improve coordination and readiness.
  6. Establish mutual aid agreements with neighboring states to enhance surge capacity for large-scale cyber events.
  7. Implement automated threat intelligence sharing with real-time indicators of compromise and tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) distributed to all participating organizations.

Objective 2.3

Lead and coordinate the protection of New Jersey's election infrastructure from cyber and physical threats and foreign influence operations.

Action Items:

  1. Maintain continuous monitoring of election infrastructure with real-time detection and rapid response capabilities.
  2. Conduct pre-election security assessments and penetration tests in coordination with county election officials and vendors.
  3. Deliver election-specific threat intelligence to state and county officials and relevant partners.
  4. In coordination with federal, state, and local government partners, counter foreign disinformation and influence operations through public awareness campaigns and strategic communications.
  5. Coordinate physical security efforts, intelligence sharing, resources, and joint operations with federal, state, county, and municipal partners.
  6. Perform post-election security audits and lessons-learned reviews to strengthen future election resilience.

Objective 2.4

Prepare for Post-Quantum, AI-Driven, and Other Emerging Technological Threats.

Action Items:

  1. Develop a post-quantum cryptography roadmap prioritizing high-value and long-lifecycle systems for early migration.
  2. Conduct a cryptographic asset inventory across executive branch agencies to identify vulnerable systems.
  3. Create quantum-ready procurement requirements for new systems expected to operate beyond 2030.
  4. Establish an AI governance framework for state agencies addressing security, privacy, ethical, and bias considerations.
  5. Partner with academic and research institutions to advance quantum and AI security research and translate findings into actionable state capabilities and guidance.
  6. Develop transition guidance for local governments, small businesses, and critical infrastructure operators preparing for quantum-era challenges.
  7. Enhance detection and mitigation capabilities for AI-generated threats such as deepfakes, adversarial AI attacks, and autonomous intrusion techniques.

Objective 2.5

Build and Sustain a Skilled Cybersecurity Workforce and Resilience Culture.

Action Items:

  1. Expand NJCCIC internship, fellowship, and apprenticeship programs with pathways to full-time employment.
  2. Provide continuous professional development and training opportunities for cybersecurity staff in state and local government.
  3. Expand use of the NJCCIC Cyber Range for hands-on training, live-fire exercises, and simulation environments for both public and private sector personnel.
  4. Create a certification support program to subsidize industry-recognized credentials for public sector cybersecurity professionals.
  5. Support veterans and career-changers entering cybersecurity through mentorship, training, and placement programs.
  6. Develop targeted public awareness campaigns to build a cyber-aware citizenry, focusing on phishing, credential theft, and online fraud.

OBJECTIVE 2.6

Enhance cybersecurity through advanced technologies and innovation.

Action Items:

  1. Research, develop, support, and implement innovative processes, practices, and technologies that improve efficiency and effectiveness of New Jersey's cybersecurity efforts.
  2. Leverage cloud security capabilities including cloud-native security controls, security automation, and scalable threat detection for systems migrating to cloud platforms.
  3. Implement security orchestration, automation, and response capabilities to automate routine security tasks, accelerate incident response, and improve efficiency.
  4. Develop and deploy deception technologies including honeypots, honeytokens, and decoy systems to detect and study adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures.
  5. Implement advanced threat hunting capabilities using behavioral analytics, user and entity behavior analytics, and threat intelligence to proactively identify sophisticated threats.
  6. Leverage big data analytics and distributed computing to process massive volumes of security data and identify subtle indicators of compromise.
  7. Explore and implement advanced authentication technologies to strengthen access controls while maintaining usability and user experience.

Goals, Objectives, and Action Items: Strategic Goal 3

STRATEGIC GOAL 3: COLLABORATIVE DEFENSE AND DISTRIBUTED RESILIENCE

Foster and cultivate strategic partnerships that enable rapid information sharing, distributed defensive capabilities, and ensures every New Jersey entity - regardless of size or resources - has access to cybersecurity support.

Objective 3.1

Deepen Intergovernmental Collaboration.

Action Items:

  1. Expand collaborative mechanisms with federal partners, including the Department of Homeland Security’s Cybersecurity Infrastructure Security Agency, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the U.S. Secret Service, the National Security Agency (NSA), and sector-specific agencies.
  2. Collaborate with state, county, and municipal government entities to improve cyber capabilities and preparedness.
  3. Strengthen partnerships with neighboring states and regional alliances to enhance situational awareness and coordinated response.
  4. Coordinate with federal agencies while adapting to their supporting rather than leading role, under EO 14239.

Objective 3.2

Expand Public-Private Partnerships.

Action Items:

  1. Establish sector-specific working groups for critical industries such as energy, water, healthcare, and finance.
  2. Partner with technology vendors, cybersecurity companies, and service providers serving New Jersey clients to extend NJCCIC threat intelligence, proven defenses, and best practices.
  3. Develop partnerships with the New Jersey Business & Industry Association, chambers of commerce, industry associations, and trade groups to provide cybersecurity support and guidance.
  4. Strengthen academic partnerships for research, talent development, and knowledge transfer.

Objective 3.3

Foster Community and Civil Society Engagement.

Action Items:

  1. Collaborate with nonprofit organizations, faith-based groups, and educational institutions to extend cybersecurity awareness and resources into communities.
  2. Develop community-based cybersecurity resilience programs tailored to local needs.
  3. Encourage reporting of cyber incidents and threats to improve statewide threat intelligence.

OBJECTIVE 3.4

Enable rapid, actionable cyber threat intelligence sharing across all stakeholder tiers.

Action Items:

  1. Deploy a tiered threat intelligence platform providing technical feeds for advanced users and translated intelligence for non-technical audiences.
  2. Implement automated indicator sharing with real-time distribution to participating organizations.
  3. Create sector-specific threat briefings tailored to unique risks and concerns of each industry.
  4. Produce regular threat assessment reports for public distribution, balancing transparency with operational security.
  5. Create a “Cyber Threat Dashboard” providing real-time visibility into threat landscape affecting New Jersey.

OBJECTIVE 3.5

Democratize access to cybersecurity resources, tools, and expertise.

Action Items:

  1. Activate and operationalize the Civilian Cyber Resilience Corps to provide security assistance to eligible organizations.
  2. Support and develop college and university student-led security operations centers, cyber clinics, and Security-asa-Service options for resource-constrained entities.
  3. Develop Cyber Toolkits providing free or low-cost security tools, templates, and guidance.
  4. Create a repository of vendor-neutral security resources including policies, procedures, training materials, and assessment tools.
  5. Support the establishment of grant programs for cybersecurity improvements by local governments, small businesses, and non-profits.

OBJECTIVE 3.6

Communicate cybersecurity effectively across diverse audiences.

Action Items:

  1. Host an annual "NJ Cybersecurity Summit" bringing together stakeholders from all sectors.
  2. Conduct sector-specific conferences and workshops throughout the year.
  3. Develop a multi-channel communication strategy (web, social media, email, etc.).
  4. Create and leverage engaging third-party content including videos, infographics, podcasts, and interactive tools.
  5. Leverage trusted messengers (community leaders, clergy, educators) to reach diverse populations.
  6. Participate in non-cyber events (county fairs, business expos, community gatherings) to help mainstream cybersecurity awareness.

Critical Success Factors
The successful execution of this strategic plan will broadly depend on or be influenced by the following considerations.

Whole-of-State Commitment: All organizations and individuals have a role to play. The strategy embraces a whole-of-state collective defense model and acknowledges that it is unrealistic to expect any one person or organization to defend against nation state actors, criminal syndicates, terrorist groups, hacktivists, and others, who can launch attacks from anywhere in the world at any time of day or night. This strategy provides a more effective approach from both a cost and resiliency perspective through the adoption of a collective defense model in which organizations collaborate to detect, share intelligence, and respond to threats together in real time.

Management Endorsement and Leadership Support: It is essential that this strategic plan is endorsed at the highest levels of New Jersey State Government and that it is driven by the Governor’s Office and agency leadership, with the Director of NJOHSP and the Domestic Security Preparedness Task Force championing its implementation. Senior Officials must identify and establish clear cybersecurity priorities and provide budgetary and human resources needed to execute the strategy.

Resource Prioritization: As the threat landscape is both evolving and expanding, it is critical to continuously advance New Jersey's security, resilience and operational capacities. The prioritization and fluid allocation of key resources is necessary to maintain currency and effectively protect against and respond to significant cybersecurity incidents.

Shared Responsibility: Cybersecurity is a shared responsibility beyond New Jersey State Government alone. As cyberspace consists of a hyper-connected array of networks, systems, and devices, the cooperation of all key stakeholders – government, industry, non-government organizations, and academia – is essential to not only the success of this strategic plan, but also the public health, welfare, and safety of the citizens, economy, and public interests of the State of New Jersey and national security.

Human Capital: As cybersecurity is a highly technical and complex discipline that requires qualified and skilled human resources at sufficient staffing levels, the ability of the NJCCIC to recruit, develop, and retain talented and mission-focused personnel is critical to carrying out this strategic plan.

Funding: This strategic plan was drafted assuming that funding levels for cybersecurity would remain stable and additional investments would be made over time to address the growing threat environment and to protect the public and private institutions, critical infrastructure assets, and the citizens of New Jersey from the threat of cyberattacks.

Authorities

New Jersey Domestic Security Preparedness Act P.L. 2001, c.246 establishes a New Jersey Domestic Security Preparedness Task Force that includes the New Jersey Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness, the New Jersey National Guard, the Office of Emergency Management in the Division of State Police, among other state, county, and local organizations in order to maximize, enhance, and effectuate coordination of the disaster preparedness and recovery resources. Included in the duties of the task force is the development, implementation, and management of comprehensive responses to any terrorist attack or any other technological disaster and the effective administration, management, and coordination of remediation and recovery actions and responses following any such attack or disaster.

State of New Jersey Executive Order No. 5 signed by Governor Corzine on March 16, 2006 establishes the New Jersey Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness as the State Agency responsible for administering, coordinating, leading, and supervising New Jersey's counterterrorism and preparedness efforts. NJOHSP is led by a Director, who also acts as the State’s Homeland Security Advisor and the Chair of the Domestic Security Preparedness Task Force. The Director and the NJOHSP shall be authorized to call upon the expertise and assistance of all State departments, divisions, and agencies to carry out their mission. The NJOHSP may, to the extent not inconsistent with any other law, employ, consult, and contract with private and public entities, and enter into such agreements with public and private individuals or entities as necessary to further the mission of the Office or of other offices and units that fall under the Director's supervision.

State of New Jersey Executive Order No. 178 signed by Governor Christie on May 20, 2015 establishes the New Jersey Cybersecurity & Communications Integration Cell (NJCCIC) as a component organization within the Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness that acts as the central State civilian interface authorized to coordinate cybersecurity information sharing and analysis across all levels of government, agencies, authorities, and the private sector pursuant to 6 U.S.C. § 133 et seq. The NJCCIC is authorized to draw upon the assistance of any department, office, division, or agency of this State to supply it with expertise and assistance, including information and personnel, to carry out the NJCCIC mission. The NJCCIC is composed of representatives of State entities, including the Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness, the Division of State Police, and the Office of Information Technology.

State of New Jersey Technology Circular 17-00-NJOIT, October 14, 2017, establishes a management structure for information security across the executive branch of New Jersey State Government including the roles and responsibilities of the Director of the Office of Homeland Security and Preparedness, the State Chief Technology Officer, the State Chief Information Security Officer, and the Director of the New Jersey Cybersecurity & Communications Integration Cell.
